## Abstract

Founded by Octavian Augustus to commemorate his victory at Actium, Nicopolis stands as a paradigmatic imperial city in mainland Greece. This paper examines the formation and development of the city from the late first century B.C.E. through the third century C.E., adopting a synthetic approach that integrates urban planning, civic institutions, cult practices, and public life.

Particular attention is paid to the city’s legal status and its relationship with central imperial authority, as well as to the organization and operation of political and religious institutions. The Actian Games occupied a central place in Nicopolis’ monumental and civic landscape and constituted the city’s principal agonistic institution during the imperial period. Enjoying panhellenic and empire-wide renown, the Actia were closely tied to the ideological foundations of the Augustan foundation. Traditional cults are documented through epigraphic evidence and civic coinage, whose iconography functioned as a medium for religious and political messaging.

Imperial cult is addressed through both long-known epigraphic testimonies and new archaeological evidence. An architectural complex currently under excavation may be identified as the city’s Sebasteion, based on its topographical context, architectural characteristics, and functional associations, as well as by comparison with parallel monuments, such as the Sebasteion at Lyktos/Lyttos in Crete.

The paper also considers aspects of urban life and social differentiation, including public buildings, the agora, the gymnasium, benefaction, professional activities, and funerary practices. Drawing on thirty years of field research, it presents Nicopolis as a dynamic urban center in which urban form, institutions, and cult practices together articulated Roman imperial authority and local civic identity.
